Communications surveillance · Relationship graph

See who talks to whom, on every channel.

A relationship graph for communications compliance. It maps employees, external contacts, messaging accounts, phone numbers and trades as one network, so an analyst can spot the backchannel before reading a single message.

Open the explorer Read the data model

Three jobs the graph does

Reading messages one at a time hides the shape of a relationship. The graph puts the shape first, then lets you drop down to the conversation.

  • Behavioral signals

    Frequency spikes, unmonitored channels, and contact across an information barrier, each tied to the relationship that tripped it and explained in words.

  • Interaction clusters

    Group the graph by team or firm to see silos, the people who bridge them, and the outsider who is closer to a desk than anyone expected.

  • Contextual threads

    Follow one conversation as it starts on Bloomberg, slips onto WhatsApp, and ends in a booking email, in order, on one timeline.

What this version does differently

I worked on relationship mapping inside an archiving and surveillance product. This is a ground-up rethink of the explorer, built around the analyst’s questions rather than the database.

Fifty nodes, on purpose
The view never draws more than 50 resources. When more match, they’re ranked by signals and connection strength, and the page tells you how many are hidden and how to reach them.
You choose the resource type
Look at people, or switch to the accounts, phone numbers and trades behind them. An unattributed WhatsApp number only shows up when you look at accounts.
Shape and line, not color
Each resource type has its own shape and fill. Monitored channels are solid lines, unmonitored ones are dashed. Color is saved for warnings.
Every view has a table and a summary
The same data is a keyboard-navigable graph, a relationships table, and a written summary, so people who read differently get the same answer.
Center on anyone
Start from a person, an account or a trade, and the graph rebuilds around them, nearest connections first, up to the limit you set.
Signals you can explain
Every flag says what tripped it, by how much, and which messages sit behind it. No black-box scores.
Arrows show who’s talking
Every line has an arrowhead at each end, sized by how many messages went that way. A one-sided relationship has a single arrow.
A timeline behind every line
Select a relationship to see daily volume against its own mean and standard deviation, when messages were sent, which days and messages fall outside the band, and the conversation itself with policy flags highlighted.

      Data model

      Nodes, edges, and the properties that make signals possible.

      Under the explorer is a property graph: resources are nodes, interactions are edges, and every edge carries enough detail to explain why it was flagged.

      Node types

      These are the resource types you can switch on and off in the explorer. Corporate email and Teams identities fold into the person; separate account nodes are only for channels where one person can hold several identities.

      ShapeTypeDrawn asKey properties

      Edge types

      Communication edges are aggregated per pair and channel for the window you’re looking at. Structural edges tie identities and trades back to people.

      EdgeBetweenDrawn as
      MESSAGEDPeople, accounts, phone numbersSolid line, thicker with volume. Dashed when part of the traffic isn’t captured.
      CALLEDPeople, phone numbersSolid line. Voice on recorded lines counts as captured.
      MET_WITHPeopleSolid line (Zoom meetings).
      SHARED_FILE_WITHPeopleListed on the relationship; adds to its weight.
      OWNS · USESPerson → account · person → phone numberDotted line.
      EXECUTED · MENTIONEDEmployee → trade · person → tradeDotted line.

      Edge properties

      PropertyWhy it’s there
      channel, countVolume per channel this window. Drives line weight and the channel bars in the inspector.
      priorCountSame pair, previous window. A pair at 2.5× its prior volume (and at least 15 messages), or a new pair at 5 or more, is a frequency spike.
      capturedWhether the employee’s side of the channel is registered for capture. Uncaptured traffic is an unmonitored-channel signal.
      timestampOrders messages into threads and sets the window.
      sentimentA score from −1 to 1, shown as context, never used alone to flag.
      translatedMarks messages machine-translated before review, so analysts know to check the source language.
      threadIdLinks messages across channels into one conversation. A thread that crosses from captured to uncaptured channels is a channel hop.
      wallCrossingAn approved exception between sides of an information barrier. Without one, research-to-trading contact is flagged.

      Why fifty

      A force layout past about fifty nodes becomes a hairball you can’t read, a keyboard user can’t traverse, and a screen reader can’t summarize. So the API is paged at 50, and the interface treats the limit as a feature: rank, show, and say what’s hidden.

      Cypher · research-to-trading contact without a wall-crossing

      // Last 30 days, strongest pairs first, one page
MATCH (r:Employee {dept: "Research"})-[m:MESSAGED|CALLED]-(t:Employee)
WHERE t.dept IN ["Equity Trading", "Fixed Income"]
  AND m.timestamp > datetime() - duration("P30D")
  AND NOT (r)-[:WALL_CROSSED {status: "approved"}]-(t)
RETURN r.name, t.name, m.channel, count(m) AS messages
ORDER BY messages DESC
LIMIT 50

      GraphQL · one page of a neighborhood, Relay connection style

      query Neighborhood($id: ID!, $types: [ResourceType!], $after: String) {
  node(id: $id) {
    id
    relationships(first: 50, after: $after, types: $types) {
      totalCount
      edges {
        cursor
        node { id label __typename }
        channels { name count priorCount captured }
        signals { type severity summary }
      }
      pageInfo { hasNextPage endCursor }
    }
  }
}

      totalCount is what lets the explorer say “showing 50 of 312” instead of silently dropping the rest.

      Accessibility

      A network diagram you can use without seeing it.

      Graphs are usually the least accessible thing on a compliance screen. These are the rules this one follows, aiming at WCAG 2.2 AA with VoiceOver, NVDA, JAWS and TalkBack.

      Three ways to read one view

      Same filters, same 50 resources, three forms. Switching tabs never changes the data.

      • 01 · GraphSpatialFor spotting clusters and bridges at a glance. Fully keyboard-operable, with what a screen reader hears shown underneath.
      • 02 · TableRelationalEvery relationship in view as a row: both ends, edge types, channels, volume, change and signals. Names select the resource.
      • 03 · SummaryNarrativeA written read-out of the view: what’s in it, who is most connected, where the signals are, and what to look at first.

      Keyboard

      The graph is one tab stop. Inside it, focus moves spatially, so the arrow you press is the direction you travel.

      KeyIn the graph
      TabEnter the graph on the selected resource, or the highest-ranked one. Tab again to leave.
      ← ↑ → ↓Move to the nearest resource in that direction.
      Enter or SpaceSelect the focused resource; details open alongside.
      N · Shift NNext or previous connection of the focused resource, busiest first.
      CCenter the graph on the focused resource.
      EOpen the timeline for the relationship between the selected and focused resources, or the focused resource’s busiest one.
      HomeJump to the highest-ranked resource.
      + · − · 0Zoom in, zoom out, fit.
      EscClear the selection.

      On a Mac trackpad: click to select, pinch to zoom, click and drag the background to pan. Two-finger scrolling still scrolls the page.

      Rules

      • Nothing depends on color. Resource types differ by shape and fill pattern; captured and uncaptured traffic differ by solid and dashed lines; signals add a dashed ring and a diamond on the line.
      • Every resource has a full name. For example: “Lando Calrissian, employee, Equity Trading. 9 connections in view. 4 signals.”
      • Changes are announced once. Filters, limits and selection each produce one polite announcement, shown in the strip under the graph.
      • The cap is spoken. When resources are hidden, the status and the announcement both say how many and how they were ranked.
      • Motion is optional. Layout changes animate briefly, and not at all with reduced motion on.
      • High contrast holds. Shapes and lines use the text color, so Windows High Contrast keeps them visible.
      • Targets are generous. Each node has a hit area larger than its shape, and each line a 14-pixel hit band.
